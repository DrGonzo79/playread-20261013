## Customer and painful status quo

The initial buyer is a small or mid-sized game publisher, gaming fund, or studio acquisition team evaluating playable indie prototypes. A scout may see 500+ submissions and sign roughly ten. Promising teams enter repeated build-review loops while technical producers, scouts, lawyers, and commercial analysts each assess different fragments. There is no consistent, signable read of the build itself.

The painful job is not “detect AI.” It is deciding whether to sign, investigate, or reject a prototype while the publisher is risking a roughly $1M development commitment. The report cites 18,000 Steam releases in 2024, 403 indie publishers worldwide, and falling per-title budgets as reasons each greenlight must carry more evidence.

## Wedge and why now

The wedge is a seven-day, standardized greenlight memo covering code health, asset provenance, playtest stability, production-plan credibility, and commercial risk. The free lead magnet is a one-page Prototype Risk Snapshot; the paid memo is $4,500; a three-day rush is $7,500; a publisher retainer is $7,500/month for three memos; later continuity includes $1,200 milestone reads and $12K/year benchmark access.

AI-disclosed Steam games reportedly grew from about 1,000 in April 2024 to 7,818 by mid-2025. The U.S. Copyright Office’s January 2025 copyrightability report and subsequent case law made asset provenance a deal risk, while Valve’s January 2026 narrowing of disclosures weakens “AI detection” as a standalone product. The durable framing is whole-prototype underwriting.

## Competitor map

- **Digital Advisors:** direct incumbent; bespoke Game Development Risk Assessments in 4–8 weeks, with named publisher/investor clients and unpublished pricing.
- **The Powell Group:** scouting, viability, pitch, and acquisition services with strong publisher relationships.
- **GameDiscoverCo:** commercial discoverability data and publisher advisory, not build-level technical diligence.
- **GameAcquire:** deal marketplace and workflow, but explicitly leaves verification to the parties.
- **Game Scout:** publisher directory and matching tools, not diligence.
- **Beck Interactive Partners:** developer-side representation and publisher access.
- **Fenwick, Reed Smith, Wilson Sonsini:** legal diligence at $500–$1,500/hour without opening the game build.

The gap is speed, standardization, and a comparable decision format. The incumbent risk is obvious: consulting firms can copy the format; the potential moat is a defensible benchmark corpus after hundreds of reviewed prototypes.

## Demand signals and evidence limits

- 403 publishers reportedly publish indie games; 150+ are profiled by Game Scout.
- Submission rejection reportedly exceeds 95%, and scouts describe repeated build cycles.
- A named incumbent sells this work and has named clients, proving category spend.
- Consultants reportedly charge $10K–$40K per project or $4K–$25K/month.
- All solution-specific search terms in the report showed zero monthly searches; distribution is warm intros and outbound, not SEO.

The evidence is secondary and vendor-reported. It does not prove that a publisher will trust PlayRead with an unreleased build or pay $4,500 for this exact memo. No first-party interview, paid pilot, or audited conversion data is available.

## Product and GTM implications

Start with a fictional-data, deterministic risk memo that makes evidence and uncertainty explicit. Sell a concierge engagement before automating repository ingestion. Target ex-publisher scouts and technical producers with existing relationships, then work a named list of 50 publishers. Offer the free snapshot only under NDA and secure compute. The CTA to test is: “Bring one rejected-or-borderline build; receive a 24-hour risk snapshot, then decide whether the full $4,500 memo is worth buying.”

## Biggest risks

1. Publishers refuse third-party build access because one leak is existential.
2. A pure SaaS founder without game-publishing trust gets ignored.
3. The addressable buyer base caps the business near lifestyle scale.
4. Incumbents copy a standardized memo before a benchmark moat exists.
5. False certainty from heuristics causes a bad greenlight or unfair rejection.
6. Copyright and platform rules change; legal conclusions require qualified counsel.
